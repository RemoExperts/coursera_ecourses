A waveform. You hear a voice, a room, a tone. You know instantly that it's speech.
Why this matters for your job

Nothing in that list of numbers says "this is a word", "this is a second speaker", or "this is a doorbell". Every one of those facts is added by a person.

The closest comparison is image labelling. A vision annotator draws a box around an object in a grid of pixels. You mark where in time a sound starts and stops, on a one-dimensional timeline. What you're paid for is precision about time and consistency about naming — not listening ability.

      The rule: if two careful people disagree, suspect the guideline before you suspect the people. Low agreement is an ambiguity signal, not a discipline problem.
      What do I do when the guideline doesn't cover it?

      Do: label it the closest defensible way → flag it → raise it. The rule then gets written once, for everybody.

      Don't: invent a private convention. Once files are merged, a private convention is indistinguishable from an error — and it teaches the model that one sound maps to two answers, which is worse than a missing label.

      Two things this means for you personally

      1. Metadata isn't paperwork. The age band, accent, device and condition you record are what make this gap measurable at all. Audits of widely used audio datasets have found weak documentation of demographics, provenance and consent — which is exactly why these gaps go undetected.

      2. Don't polish your accent. When a brief asks for a specific dialect or register, that's a technical requirement. Over-formalising your speech removes the exact variation the dataset was commissioned to capture.
